# Conference CFP Tips and Reference

A comprehensive reference for conference Call for Proposals (CFP) submissions. Covers what reviewers look for, common mistakes, templates, and a detailed directory of major conferences with tactical advice for each.

---

## Conference Directory

Word limits, session formats, review processes, timelines, and tactical tips for 27 conferences. Always verify against the current CFP form before submitting, as limits and formats change year to year.

### KubeCon / CloudNativeCon (CNCF)

- **Abstract:** 200-300 words. **Description:** 900 words max.
- **Session types:** Session Presentation (30 min, 1-2 speakers), Panel Discussion (30 min, 3-5 speakers), Tutorial (75 min, 1-5 speakers), Lightning Talk (5 min, 1 speaker).
- **Review process:** Blind review. Each submission scored by multiple program committee members on technical merit, relevance, clarity, and novelty. Speakers limited to 3 submissions total; exceeding this may result in all submissions being removed.
- **What reviewers prioritize:** Relevance to cloud native ecosystem, end-user production stories, novelty (reviewers check past schedules), connection to specific CNCF projects.
- **Acceptance rate:** 9-11% in recent years (2,541 submissions, 223 accepted for EU 2024).
- **CFP timeline:** NA typically closes late May/early June for a November conference. EU typically closes in October for a March/April conference.
- **Tactical tip:** Co-present with an end-user customer. CNCF explicitly prioritizes end-user perspectives over vendor-driven content. A vendor plus end-user pairing consistently scores higher than two vendor employees.

### Red Hat Summit

- **Abstract:** 200 words. **Description:** 500 words.
- **Session types:** Breakout presentation (40 min), Interview/panel, Hands-on lab, Expo hall theater (short form), Lightning Talk (10 min). Session types may be adjusted during selection.
- **Review process:** Red Hat subject matter experts review and rate each proposal. Not blind. Reviewers look for original, well-written abstracts, compelling topics, and clear takeaways.
- **What reviewers prioritize:** Advanced technical content (explicitly stated for 2026), business impact and outcomes, customer and partner co-presentations, cross-product integration stories (e.g., OpenShift + Ansible + RHEL).
- **Acceptance rate:** Not publicly disclosed.
- **CFP timeline:** Typically opens in October, closes in November for a May conference. Notifications in February.
- **Tactical tip:** Frame your talk around a customer outcome, not a product feature. Red Hat Summit reviewers want to see how technology solved a real business problem. Lead with the problem and the measurable result.

### DevConf.CZ

- **Abstract:** 200 words. **Description:** No strict limit.
- **Session types:** Talk (35 min, 25 min talk + 10 min Q&A), Lightning Talk (15 min), Workshop (80 min).
- **Review process:** CfP committee reviews holistically across tracks. Regional priority for Europe, Middle East, and Africa speakers. Limit of 3 submissions per person (including co-speaker credits). AI-generated submissions are rejected.
- **What reviewers prioritize:** Technical depth (not overviews), new or planned work, emerging topics 1-2 years out, audience interaction and demos. Product pitches score poorly.
- **Acceptance rate:** Approximately 25% for talks. Limit of one 35-minute talk per speaker.
- **CFP timeline:** Opens mid-December, closes early March for a June conference.
- **Tactical tip:** DevConf is free and community-driven. If this is your first talk, say so in your submission. New speakers are actively welcomed, and the committee values fresh perspectives from contributors.

### DevConf.US

- **Abstract:** 200 words. **Description:** No strict limit.
- **Session types:** Talk (35 min), Workshop (60-90 min), Lightning Talk (15 min).
- **Review process:** Similar to DevConf.CZ but with regional priority for the Americas.
- **What reviewers prioritize:** Upstream and community focus, hands-on content, contributor stories.
- **Acceptance rate:** Not publicly disclosed, but generally higher than DevConf.CZ due to fewer submissions.
- **CFP timeline:** Typically opens early in the year for a summer/fall conference.
- **Tactical tip:** Workshops are popular and tend to have a higher acceptance rate than standard talks. If your content is hands-on, submit it as a workshop.

### PyCon US

- **Abstract:** 300 words. **Outline:** 400 words.
- **Session types:** Talk (30 min, 5 min Q&A included), Tutorial (3 hours, separate submission), Poster, Charla (Spanish-language track, 2 full days), Lightning Talk (5 min). Limit of 3 submissions per person, but only 1 talk accepted per speaker.
- **Review process:** Community reviewers score proposals. Outline quality is heavily weighted. Speaker experience is explicitly deprioritized. Proposal Mentorship Program available for early submitters.
- **What reviewers prioritize:** Accessibility to a broad Python audience, unique or underrepresented topics, strong outline with clear progression, clear value for attendees.
- **Acceptance rate:** Highly competitive. Not officially published as a percentage.
- **CFP timeline:** Typically closes in December for a May conference.
- **Tactical tip:** Submit early and opt into the Proposal Mentorship Program. Mentors provide feedback on your draft before the deadline closes. This is one of the few conferences that offers structured help before you submit.

### FOSDEM

- **Abstract:** 200 words. **Description:** Varies by devroom.
- **Session types:** Talk (20-50 min, varies by devroom), Lightning Talk (15 min). Main track is invite-only.
- **Review process:** Each devroom has its own independent review committee. No centralized scoring rubric. All speakers must present in person in Brussels. No remote presentations allowed.
- **What reviewers prioritize:** Technical depth (this is a practitioner audience), upstream and FOSS focus, devroom fit (read the devroom description carefully), practical and actionable content.
- **Acceptance rate:** Varies widely by devroom. Popular devrooms can be very competitive.
- **CFP timeline:** Devroom proposals due mid-November. Talk submissions to devrooms typically due late November to early December for a late January/early February conference.
- **Tactical tip:** Submit to the right devroom. A strong talk in the wrong devroom gets rejected. Read the devroom's description and past schedules before submitting. If your talk spans two devrooms, pick the one where the core audience lives.

### Open Source Summit (Linux Foundation)

- **Abstract:** 200 words. **Description:** 600 words.
- **Session types:** Session (30 min), Tutorial (90 min), BoF (60 min), Lightning Talk (5-10 min). Panels require all speaker names at submission. Limit of 2 submissions per speaker.
- **Review process:** Program committee review. All-male panels are not accepted. Submissions relying heavily on AI-generated content are flagged. Previously presented talks (at CNCF or LF events within the past year) are not accepted.
- **What reviewers prioritize:** Vendor-neutral content, open source focus, practical value, diversity of speakers.
- **Acceptance rate:** Not publicly disclosed.
- **CFP timeline:** NA typically closes in February for a June conference. EU typically closes in June for an October conference.
- **Tactical tip:** The Linux Foundation explicitly states that AI-generated or templated submissions lack the specificity needed for acceptance. Write your own proposal with concrete, specific details from your actual experience.

### AnsibleFest / Ansible Community Events

- **Session types:** Breakout (40 min), Workshop (90 min), Lightning Talk (10 min).
- **Review process:** Review by Ansible community and Red Hat subject matter experts.

### All Things Open

## What Reviewers Actually Prioritize

Based on publicly available reviewer guidelines from major conferences.

### KubeCon / CloudNativeCon (CNCF)

The CNCF publishes reviewer guidelines emphasizing:
- **Relevance to cloud native ecosystem.** Proposals must connect to CNCF projects, cloud native patterns, or the Kubernetes ecosystem specifically. Generic infrastructure talks that do not mention the cloud native stack score lower.
- **End-user perspective.** CNCF actively prioritizes real-world production stories over vendor-driven content. Proposals from end users or featuring end-user co-presenters score higher.
- **Novelty over repetition.** Reviewers check past KubeCon schedules. If the same topic was covered last cycle, your version must bring new data, a new angle, or updated results.
- **Blind review.** Your abstract must stand on its own without relying on your name or employer to carry it.
- **Scoring rubric:** Technical merit, relevance to the track, clarity of the abstract, and novelty. Each dimension is scored independently.

### FOSDEM

FOSDEM devroom organizers have shared these priorities:
- **Technical depth.** FOSDEM audiences are practitioners who build the software. Surface-level overviews get rejected. Show code, architecture, or protocol-level detail.
- **Upstream focus.** FOSDEM prioritizes free and open source software contributions. Talks about proprietary products or vendor platforms are consistently rejected.
- **Devroom fit.** Each devroom has its own review committee and scope. A great talk submitted to the wrong devroom gets rejected. Read the devroom description carefully.
- **Practical value.** Reviewers prefer talks where the audience learns a technique, discovers a tool, or gains insight they can use immediately.

### PyCon US

PyCon publishes detailed reviewer guidelines:
- **Accessibility.** Proposals should be understandable to a broad Python audience. Reviewers penalize unnecessary jargon and assumed knowledge that is not listed in prerequisites.
- **Diversity of topics.** PyCon actively seeks talks that are not just web frameworks and data science. Unique topics or underrepresented areas of Python score well.
- **Speaker experience is secondary.** PyCon explicitly tells reviewers not to weigh speaker experience heavily. A first-time speaker with a clear, well-scoped proposal beats a veteran with a vague one.
- **Outline quality.** PyCon reviewers read your outline carefully. A strong outline with clear progression signals a well-prepared talk.

### Red Hat Summit

Red Hat subject matter experts review each proposal looking for:
- **Advanced technical content.** Red Hat Summit 2026 explicitly asked for content that goes beyond the basics, offering deeper insights and more technical detail.
- **Original, well-written abstracts.** Clear writing signals a clear thinker. Reviewers look for compelling topics with obvious takeaways.
- **Customer and partner stories.** Co-presentations with customers or partners are strongly encouraged. Real-world adoption stories carry more weight than theoretical content.
- **Cross-product integration.** Talks that show how multiple Red Hat technologies work together (OpenShift + Ansible + RHEL, for example) are valued for demonstrating ecosystem breadth.
- **Business impact.** Connect technical decisions to measurable business outcomes whenever possible.

### SREcon (USENIX)

USENIX publishes detailed proposal guidelines:
- **Blind initial review.** Program committee scores proposals based only on title, descriptions, and notes to committee. Speaker names and affiliations are hidden.
- **Clear problem statements.** Your abstract must answer: What is the problem? Why does it matter now? What will the audience take away?
- **Lessons from failures.** SREcon explicitly encourages talks about what went wrong, not just what went right. Incident postmortems and hard-won lessons are valued.
- **Principled reasoning.** Explain the principles behind your solution, not just the solution itself. How did those principles guide your design?
- **Abstracts are not teasers.** The abstract must contain real information about the content. Vague promises to "reveal" or "explore" score poorly.
- **Title and abstract consistency.** Reviewers noted cases where the title conveyed a significantly different tone than the descriptions. Make sure they match.

### General Patterns Across All Conferences

Ranked from most to least impactful based on consistent patterns across reviewer guidelines:

1. **Clear, specific value proposition.** The reviewer should know within 30 seconds what the attendee will learn. This is the single most important factor.
2. **Evidence of real experience.** Production usage, specific numbers, actual project names. Generic descriptions of what "could" be done lose to stories of what was done.
3. **Appropriate scope.** A focused, achievable scope beats an ambitious-sounding but unrealistic one every time.
4. **Relevance to the specific conference.** Tailor your submission. Reviewers can tell when a proposal was submitted to multiple conferences without modification.
5. **Actionable takeaways.** Talks that end with "now you can do X" beat talks that end with "now you know about X."
6. **Novelty.** Reviewers check past schedules. If the topic was covered recently, your version must bring something new.
7. **Clean writing.** Typos, unclear sentences, and jargon-heavy abstracts signal a talk that may also be poorly prepared.
8. **Not a vendor pitch.** Every conference flags this. Even if you work for a vendor, frame the talk around the problem and the open source solution.
9. **"Would I attend this talk?"** The most common question reviewers ask themselves. Write your abstract so the answer is obviously yes.

## Common Rejection Reasons

Fourteen patterns that consistently lead to rejected proposals, based on reviewer feedback from multiple conferences.

1. **Too vague.** The abstract reads like a marketing blurb with no concrete content. "In this talk, we will explore the exciting world of..." tells the reviewer nothing.

2. **Too broad.** Trying to cover everything about a large topic in one session. Pick one angle and go deep rather than skimming five angles at surface level.

3. **No clear takeaway.** The proposal describes what you will talk about but not what the audience will learn or be able to do afterward. Every proposal needs a "so what."

4. **Wrong audience fit.** Submitting a deep-dive to a beginner track, or a 101-level talk to an advanced track. Read the track description before selecting it.

5. **Duplicate content.** The same talk has been given at multiple conferences without updates. Reviewers check, and some conferences (CNCF, Linux Foundation) explicitly reject talks presented within the last year.

6. **Vendor pitch.** The talk is a thinly disguised product demo. Conference audiences and reviewers reject these quickly. Even if your company sponsors the event, frame the content around problems and open source solutions.

7. **Weak learning objectives.** "Understand X" is not a measurable objective. "Evaluate X against Y for your production workload" is. Reviewers who see vague objectives assume the talk will also be vague.

8. **Poor writing quality.** Typos, grammar issues, and unclear sentences signal a talk that may also be poorly prepared. If you do not proofread your 200-word abstract, reviewers doubt you will prepare a 30-minute talk well.

9. **Abstract that reads like a table of contents.** "First I will cover A, then B, then C, and finally D." This tells the reviewer the structure but nothing about the substance. Replace the outline with a narrative that conveys the key insight.

10. **No evidence the speaker has actually done this work.** The proposal describes a project or approach in generic terms without mentioning specific results, environments, or lessons learned. Reviewers look for proof that you built, deployed, or operated what you are proposing to discuss.

11. **Proposal that was clearly generated by AI.** Multiple conferences now flag this explicitly. AI-generated proposals tend to be generic, use filler phrases, and lack the specific details that come from real experience. DevConf.CZ rejects fully AI-generated submissions outright. Open Source Summit warns that AI-generated content "lacks the specificity needed to evaluate technical depth."

12. **Proposal that ignores the conference's specific audience.** Submitting a Python data science talk to a systems programming conference, or a deep Kubernetes talk to a Python conference. Every rejection like this wastes a reviewer's time and signals that you did not read the CFP description.

13. **Over-reliance on acronyms without context.** Using "RBAC," "CRD," "SLO," "MTTR," and "gRPC" in a single abstract without defining any of them. Your abstract should be readable by any reviewer on the committee, not just the ones who happen to know your stack.

14. **Title and abstract mismatch.** SREcon reviewers specifically noted cases where "the title conveyed a significantly different tone than the descriptions." If your title promises a war story but your abstract describes a product overview, reviewers notice the disconnect.

## Templates for Different Talk Formats

Fill-in-the-blank templates for common session types. Replace all [bracketed placeholders] with your specific content.

### Standard Talk (30-40 minutes)

**Title:** [Action verb] [specific thing]: [outcome or surprising detail]

Example format: "Migrating 200 services to gRPC: what the latency numbers actually showed"

**Abstract:**

[One sentence describing the problem you faced or the question you set out to answer.]

## The Submission Checklist

Organized by phase. Check each item before moving to the next stage.

### Before Writing

- [ ] Read the conference CFP page completely, including the FAQ and any linked guidelines.
- [ ] Check the conference's past schedules (usually published on their website). Has your exact topic been covered recently? If yes, identify what is new or different about your angle.
- [ ] Identify the right track or devroom for your talk. Read the track description and confirm your content matches.
- [ ] Confirm the session type matches your content depth. A 5-minute lightning talk and a 90-minute workshop require very different proposals.
- [ ] Decide on your single core takeaway. If you cannot state it in one sentence, your scope is too broad.
- [ ] Check if the conference has a mentorship or proposal review program (PyCon US, DjangoCon, EuroPython) and opt in if available.
- [ ] Check the submission limit per speaker. Many conferences cap at 2-3 proposals.

### While Writing

- [ ] First sentence hooks the reader with a problem, a surprising fact, or a bold claim.
- [ ] Abstract includes specific details: numbers, project names, technologies, environments.
- [ ] Every acronym is defined at least once, or is universally known to the target audience (e.g., HTTP, API).
- [ ] Learning objectives use action verbs (Evaluate, Design, Implement, Troubleshoot, Compare, Build) not passive verbs (Understand, Learn, Know, Appreciate).
- [ ] Abstract is written in first person or second person ("I will show" or "You will learn"), not third person ("Attendees will be presented with").
- [ ] Title is under 75 characters and uses concrete nouns and verbs.
- [ ] Word count is within the conference's stated limit.
- [ ] Proposal does not read like a product pitch. If a product is mentioned, the focus is on the problem it solves and the open source approach.
- [ ] No buzzwords: "revolutionary," "cutting-edge," "game-changing," "deep dive into the landscape."
- [ ] Abstract tells a narrative (problem, approach, result, takeaway), not a table of contents.
- [ ] Speaker bio connects to this specific talk's topic.
- [ ] Prerequisites are honest about required skill level.
- [ ] Time allocations in the outline are realistic for the session length.

### Before Submitting

- [ ] Read the abstract out loud. Does it flow naturally?
- [ ] Ask a colleague who is NOT in your domain to read it. Do they understand the value?
- [ ] Verify the session type, track, and tags are correctly selected in the form.
- [ ] Speaker bio is tailored to this specific talk (see tips below).
- [ ] If multi-speaker, all speaker names and bios are included (many conferences require this at submission time).
- [ ] Check for typos and grammar issues one more time.
- [ ] If the conference flags AI-generated content, ensure your proposal has the specific details and personal voice that distinguish it from generated text.
- [ ] Confirm you have not exceeded the submission limit per speaker.
- [ ] Verify any links in the description are working.
- [ ] Save a copy of your submission text outside the CFP platform in case you need it later.

### After Submitting

- [ ] Note the notification date on your calendar.
- [ ] If the CFP platform allows edits before the deadline, revisit your submission after 48 hours with fresh eyes and revise if needed.
- [ ] If you receive reviewer feedback (some conferences offer this), revise accordingly.
- [ ] Start preparing slides even before you hear back. If accepted, you will be glad you started early. If rejected, the slides become a blog post or a submission to another conference.
- [ ] If rejected, read any feedback provided and revise the proposal for the next conference.
- [ ] Track your submissions across conferences in a spreadsheet: conference name, submission date, talk title, notification date, result.

## Tips for Stronger Submissions

### Title

- Keep it under 75 characters when possible.
- Use concrete nouns and verbs. "Building," "Debugging," "Migrating" are stronger than "Exploring" or "Discussing."
- Avoid questions as titles unless the question is genuinely provocative. "What if your CI pipeline is lying to you?" works. "Have you considered service meshes?" does not.
- Include a specific detail when possible. "Scaling to 10,000 pods" is more compelling than "Scaling Kubernetes."

### Abstract

- First sentence should hook. Start with a problem, a surprising fact, or a bold claim.
- Middle section should describe your approach or content with specific details.
- Last sentence should promise a clear outcome.
- Write in first person or second person. "You will learn" is more engaging than "Attendees will be presented with."
- Include at least one specific number, project name, or concrete result.

### Learning Objectives

- Use Bloom's taxonomy action verbs: Evaluate, Design, Implement, Troubleshoot, Compare, Build.
- Avoid: Understand, Learn, Know, Appreciate. These are not measurable.
- Tie each objective to something the attendee can do in their own work the next week.

### Speaker Bio

Write a bio that builds credibility for this specific talk, not a generic bio you reuse everywhere.

- Lead with your most relevant credential for this topic. If your talk is about Kubernetes migrations, lead with the fact that you migrated 40 services, not that you have 15 years of experience in software.
- Mention your employer and role, but do not make it the focus.
- Include one human detail: an open source project you maintain, a community you are part of, something that makes you memorable.
- Keep it to 2-3 sentences. Reviewers read hundreds of bios. Respect their time.
- If you are a first-time speaker, say so plainly. Many conferences value new voices, and hiding it helps no one.
- Write a different bio for each submission. A bio about your Kubernetes experience does not belong on a Python community talk.

**Example of a weak bio:** "Jane is a Senior Staff Engineer at Acme Corp with 15 years of experience in distributed systems, cloud computing, and DevOps. She is passionate about open source."

**Example of a strong bio for a Kubernetes migration talk:** "Jane led the migration of Acme Corp's 40-service platform from VMs to Kubernetes over 8 months, which is the project she will discuss in this talk. She maintains the open source migration toolkit she built during the project (github.com/jane/migrate-kit) and organizes the Portland Kubernetes meetup."

### Handling Multi-Speaker Proposals

- Clarify each speaker's role in the proposal. Reviewers want to know who covers what, not just that two people will be on stage.
- The strongest multi-speaker proposals pair complementary perspectives: a developer and an operator, a vendor engineer and a customer, a maintainer and an end user.
- Many conferences limit travel reimbursement per submission, not per speaker. RustConf explicitly notes that "travel funding is budgeted per talk, not per speaker." Confirm the speaker support policy before proposing a multi-speaker talk.
- For panels, all speaker names are almost always required at submission time. Do not submit with TBD panelists.
- Keep the total number of speakers to 2-3 for a standard talk. A panel of 5 in a 30-minute slot gives each person 6 minutes. Reviewers know that math does not work.

### When and How to Resubmit a Rejected Talk

- Read any feedback provided. If the conference does not provide feedback, ask. Some will share it if you email the program committee.
- Common fixable reasons for rejection: too broad (narrow the scope), wrong track (submit to a different one), stale topic (add new data or a new angle).
- Wait at least one conference cycle before resubmitting to the same conference. If you resubmit the identical proposal, reviewers notice.
- Revise the title and abstract meaningfully. Do not just change a few words. Address the core reason it was rejected.
- A rejected talk is not a failed talk. Some of the best conference talks were rejected on the first submission.
- Consider submitting to a different conference where the topic is a better fit. If a major conference rejected it, a smaller regional event (SCALE, All Things Open, DevConf) can be a stepping stone.

### How to Adapt One Talk for Multiple Conferences

- Start with the core content and tailor the framing for each audience. A talk about "debugging distributed tracing" becomes a Kubernetes debugging story for KubeCon, a Python instrumentation tutorial for PyCon, and an SRE incident response case study for SREcon.
- Change the title and abstract for each conference. Identical submissions to different conferences are a missed opportunity to tailor your message.
- Adjust the technical depth. A PyCon audience needs more context on infrastructure concepts. A KubeCon audience needs more context on application-level concerns.
- Highlight different takeaways. Each conference audience has different daily work. Frame the "so what" around their specific challenges.
- Check that the conference does not reject talks given elsewhere within the last year. CNCF and Linux Foundation events explicitly check for this. If your talk was recorded and published, assume reviewers have seen it.
- Track which version you submitted where. Reviewers occasionally compare notes across events.

## Conference-Specific Notes

Quick-reference notes for tailoring your submission to specific events.

### KubeCon / CloudNativeCon
- CNCF uses a blind review process. Your abstract must stand on its own.
- End-user case studies are highly valued.
- Mention specific CNCF projects (not just "cloud native tools").
- Co-presenting with an end user strengthens your submission.
- You are limited to 3 submissions. If you are on more than 3, all may be removed from review.

### Red Hat Summit
- Business impact matters. Connect technical content to measurable outcomes.
- Cross-product integration stories do well (e.g., OpenShift + Ansible + RHEL).
- Customer co-presentations are strongly encouraged.
- The 2026 CFP explicitly asked for more advanced content. Beginner overviews are less likely to be accepted.

### DevConf.CZ / DevConf.US
- Community and upstream focus. Show your contributions.
- Workshops and hands-on labs are popular and have good acceptance rates.
- New speakers are actively welcomed. If it is your first talk, say so.
- Fully AI-generated proposals are rejected. You may use AI to polish grammar, but note it in your submission.
- DevConf.CZ prioritizes European, Middle Eastern, and African speakers. DevConf.US prioritizes Americas speakers.

### PyCon US
- The community values inclusion and accessibility.
- Tutorials (3 hours) have separate submissions from talks.
- Poster sessions are a great way to start if you have not presented before.
- Charlas (Spanish-language track) is a dedicated 2-day track.
- Submit early for access to the Proposal Mentorship Program.

### FOSDEM
- Deeply technical audience. Do not oversimplify.
- Submit to a specific devroom, not the main track (main track is invite-only).
- Lightning talks are a good entry point.
- All speakers must present in person in Brussels. Remote presentations are not allowed.

### AnsibleFest / Ansible Community Events
- Real-world automation stories with measurable outcomes.
- Show your playbooks, roles, or collections. Code examples are expected.
- Operations-focused content performs well.

### SREcon
- Blind initial review. Your proposal must stand alone without your name attached.
- Failures and lessons learned are explicitly encouraged.
- Explain your reasoning, not just your solution.
- Good abstracts take time. Even experienced speakers revise multiple times.

### All Things Open
- One of the largest community-run open source conferences on the US East Coast (6,500+ attendees).
- Explicitly seeks diverse speakers and underrepresented perspectives.
- Extended workshops are increasingly important in the new format.
- Lightning talks on Monday, standard sessions on Tuesday.

### Devoxx
- The 45-minute "Conference" format is the most competitive.
- "Tools in Action" (30 min) and "Lunch Talk" (15 min) have better odds.
- Multiple Devoxx events worldwide with separate CFPs. A rejection at Devoxx France does not prevent acceptance at Devoxx Belgium.
- Videos are published on YouTube, giving your talk a second life.

### EuroPython
- Very short CFP window (approximately 2 weeks). Do not wait for the announcement to start writing.
- Submit early to get screening feedback within the submission period.
- LLM use for polishing is allowed but must be noted. Fully LLM-generated proposals score poorly.
- Community voting means your abstract should appeal broadly, not just to experts.

Note: Always check the current CFP form for the conference you are submitting to. Limits, formats, and review criteria change year to year.
